Inside an AI News Video Pipeline: From Headline to Finished Video

How our News AI Engine turns one headline into a narrated, edited, quality-checked video - every stage explained, with the trade-offs.

News AI Engine Editorial Team November 21, 2026 · 5 min read

When people hear “AI-generated news video”, they usually imagine one magic model that takes a prompt and returns a finished clip. Production reality is very different. Our News AI Engine is a chain of small, boring, testable stages - and almost every quality problem we have ever had came from the seams between them.

This article walks through the whole pipeline, the way it runs today, and explains why each stage exists.

The eight stages of the pipeline. Each stage saves its output, so a crash never forces a full restart.

The input: a human-written headline

Everything starts with a title written by a person. That is a deliberate design decision. The engine is very good at turning a headline into a structured script, but deciding what is news - and wording it fairly - is an editorial judgement we never hand to a model.

The headline is never rewritten by the system. It is shown on screen exactly as typed, and the script must address it.

Stage 1: Script generation with guard-rails

A language model writes the narration. The prompt does three jobs:

  1. Structure - a hook in the first sentence, context, two or three distinct developments, and a strong close.
  2. Length - we target a speaking time, not a word count. Our narrator voice averages about 140 words per minute, so a 65-second short needs roughly 155 words.
  3. Factual boundaries - no invented quotes, numbers, meetings, documents or court decisions. Unconfirmed claims must be attributed (“reports say…”).

The model returns structured JSON rather than free text: the narration paragraphs, plus “anchors” - exact phrases where each development begins. Those anchors become important later, when graphics must appear at the moment words are spoken.

Every response is validated. If the narration is too short, opens with filler such as “In this video…”, or contains headings and stage directions, the router rejects it and asks again - once - with the exact reason. We cover the router in detail in Building a Multi-Provider AI Router.

Stage 2: Cleaning the text before speech

A surprising number of AI scripts contain text that should never be spoken: section headings, [SCENE START], citation markers, and even notes like “I could not verify this headline”. If that reaches the text-to-speech engine, the narrator reads it aloud. A dedicated cleaner strips it and keeps any editorial warnings for a human to read. See Stop AI Preambles From Reaching Your Text-to-Speech.

Stage 3: Narration

The cleaned script goes to a synthetic voice with a fixed, approved voice profile. We do not change speed or pitch in post-production: consistency of the voice is part of the channel’s identity, and time-stretching audio makes it sound artificial.

Stage 4: Word-level timing

This is the stage most tutorials skip, and the one that makes the biggest visual difference. We run speech recognition (whisper.cpp) on the narration and align every recognised word to the known script text. The result is a timeline where each word has a start and end time - typically matching 94-97% of words directly, with the rest interpolated.

With that timeline, captions highlight the word being spoken, and a “Development 02” board appears on the exact syllable where the narrator begins that development. Full details: Forced Alignment With whisper.cpp.

Stage 5: Footage intelligence

The engine keeps a library of video clips and images per channel. A vision model describes each clip once - setting, people, on-screen text, where the main subject sits in the frame - and the results are cached. For each three-second slot in the edit, the engine scores clips against the sentence being spoken. See AI Visual Scanning of a Footage Library.

Stage 6: The visual plan

A “Visual Director” decides which graphics appear and when: name supers, location tags, typing windows, kinetic text, stat cards. It is deliberately restrained - creative graphics may cover at most about 45% of the runtime, never two in the same screen region, never the same type twice in a row. We explain why in Designing an AI Visual Director That Doesn’t Overdo It.

Stage 7: Rendering

FFmpeg does all the rendering:

  • a montage that cuts every three seconds with half-second crossfades;
  • occasional freeze-frame zooms and black-and-white moments for rhythm;
  • motion graphics drawn by libass from a generated subtitle file;
  • the narration mixed on top.

Vertical 9:16 videos and widescreen 16:9 videos use the same engine with different layouts. See Building a 3-Second Montage With FFmpeg xfade.

Stage 8: Quality control, then scheduling

Before anything can be published, about thirty automated checks run: dimensions, codecs, audio levels, long silences, black frames, caption width, development-board sync, and whether any AI meta-text survived into the narration. A failed check blocks automatic publishing. See Automated Quality Control for Generated Videos.

Publishing is handled by a database-backed scheduler with fixed daily slots and strict idempotency, covered in Scheduling Social Video Uploads Safely.

What we learned designing it this way

Persist every stage. Each stage writes its output to disk and records its status in a database. If the phone reboots in the middle of a render, the job resumes at the render - the script, voice and timing are not regenerated or paid for twice.

Validate at boundaries. Most of our worst bugs were not in a model; they were in what one stage passed to the next.

Keep humans where judgement lives. Titles, publishing decisions and fact-checking stay with people. The engine removes repetitive work; it does not remove responsibility.

Measure on real hardware. Our engine has run on an Android phone. That forced us to care about memory, render time and storage in ways a cloud server would have hidden - and made the system better.

Where to go next

If you are building something similar, start with the stage that hurts most. For most creators that is either unreliable AI responses (the router) or captions that drift out of sync (forced alignment).
