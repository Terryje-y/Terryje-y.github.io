Designing an AI Visual Director That Doesn't Overdo It

Letting AI choose video graphics is easy; stopping it from overdoing them is hard. The rules our Visual Director enforces in code.

News AI Engine Editorial Team November 21, 2026 · 4 min read

When we first let a language model plan graphics for our videos, the results were technically impressive and visually exhausting: a typing window, then kinetic text, then a full-screen quote, then an arrow - in the first fifteen seconds. Good editors know that footage needs room to breathe. This article describes how our Visual Director combines AI suggestions with hard rules so the result looks edited rather than decorated.

Two kinds of graphics

We split graphics into two groups with very different rules.

Hard-synced information graphics appear exactly when something is said, every time:

  • the numbered development board (“Development 02 - Labour pressure”) at the first word of that development;
  • a name super at the first mention of a person;
  • location and date tags when a place or date is spoken.

These come straight from the script’s structure and the word timeline. The AI cannot move them.

Creative graphics are optional emphasis: typing windows, kinetic text, full-screen punch lines, highlighter bars, notifications, arrows, timelines, comparisons and stat cards. The AI suggests these; the rules decide whether they are allowed.

What the AI is asked to do

The model receives the narration split into timed sentences and a catalogue of graphics with their requirements. For example, a typing window needs 6-16 words copied from the sentence; a stat card needs a number that is actually spoken. It returns a plan like this:

{"plan": [
  {"sentence": 3, "graphic": "stat", "value": "thirty percent", "text": "Inflation sits above", "transition": "fade"},
  {"sentence": 5, "graphic": "none", "transition": "dissolve"},
  {"sentence": 7, "graphic": "typing_window", "title": "REPORT", "text": "The National Assembly approved a supplementary budget", "transition": "whip"}
]}

The prompt explicitly asks for restraint - “leave many sentences with none so the footage carries the scene” - but we never rely on the prompt alone.

Rule 1: Never invent on-screen text

Every quoted phrase in a creative graphic must appear verbatim in the narration. If the model paraphrases or invents, that graphic is dropped:

probe = normalise(item["text"])
if probe not in normalise(narration):
    item["graphic"] = "none"

On-screen words carry authority. In news, a misquoted phrase on screen is worse than no graphic at all.

Rule 2: One element per screen region

The screen is divided into named regions - top band, top-left, top-right, middle, lower band, and “full band” for full-screen moments. Every graphic type declares the regions it occupies. Before placing a graphic, the director checks the timeline:

def free(self, start, end, regions):
    for s, e, r, _ in self.items:
        if start < e and s < end and (r & regions or "band_full" in r or "band_full" in regions):
            return False
    return True

Hard-synced graphics are placed first, so a creative graphic can never cover a development board.

Rule 3: A runtime budget

Creative graphics may cover at most 45% of a dynamic video’s runtime - 20% in the restrained broadcast style. Once the budget is spent, remaining suggestions are ignored. Our quality control also fails a video if creative graphics exceed half the runtime.

Rule 4: Breathing room and variety

  • At least 1.2 seconds between creative graphics.
  • Never the same type twice in a row.
  • Per-type caps (for example, one full-screen punch line per short), scaled up gently for long videos.
  • No creative graphics during the opening title card on widescreen videos.

Rule 5: Strong transitions are special

The director also picks transitions for the 3-second cuts. Whips and zooms are reserved for story turns, such as the start of a development; inside a thought, cuts use fades and dissolves. A strong transition is never repeated back-to-back, and roughly half of the AI’s strong-transition suggestions are softened to keep them meaningful.

Rule 6: Reproducibility

Every random choice - a variant of the headline animation, a caption style, a tie-break - uses a random generator seeded from the video’s ID. If a render fails and is retried, it produces the identical edit. This makes bugs reproducible and keeps the review copy identical to the published one.

When the AI is unavailable

Language models are sometimes overloaded. The director then falls back to a rule-based planner that uses the script’s key phrases, numbers and people. For long videos the AI plans in windows of about 28 sentences; if one window fails, the rest go straight to the rules instead of waiting on an overloaded provider again. The video is still made, still synced, just a little less inventive.

If the AI is too conservative and places fewer than three creative graphics in a short, the rule planner tops it up - within the same budget and region limits.

Styles are just different rule settings

Our three editing styles share the director and differ only in configuration:

Style Creative graphics Transitions Captions
Classic none (boards only) fades plain
Broadcast banner and stats, 20% budget wipes at developments active word highlighted
Dynamic AI-chosen, 45% budget context-aware variety active word boxed

Takeaways

  • Separate information (always synced, never optional) from emphasis (optional, budgeted).
  • Validate every AI suggestion against the narration and the screen layout.
  • Encode taste as rules: budgets, gaps, regions, variety.
  • Seed randomness per video.

Restraint is not a limitation of an automated editor. It is the feature that makes the result watchable.
