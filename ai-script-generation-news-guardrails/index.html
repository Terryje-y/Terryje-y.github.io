AI Script Generation for News Videos: Structure, Length and Factual Guard-rails

How we prompt and validate AI models to write gripping 60-second news scripts that stay factual: structured output, timed length and claim checks.

By News AI Engine Editorial Team Published November 21, 2026 4 min read

A news script has two jobs that pull in opposite directions. It must be gripping - a short video loses viewers in the first three seconds - and it must be accurate, because invented details in a news video can mislead thousands of people. This article explains how our engine asks language models for scripts that do both, and how code, not hope, enforces the rules.

Start from a human headline

The engine never decides what the story is. A person writes the headline; the system writes narration about that headline. The headline is shown on screen exactly as typed and is never rewritten by the model.

Ask for structure, not prose

Free-form text is hard to validate. We ask for JSON:

{
  "narration": ["hook paragraph", "context paragraph", "development paragraph", "..."],
  "developments": [{"headline": "COURT UPDATE", "anchor": "The court adjourned the case", "status": "reported"}],
  "people": [{"name": "…", "role": "SHORT ROLE", "anchor": "first words where named"}],
  "places": [{"name": "…", "anchor": "…"}],
  "key_phrases": [{"text": "two to five words copied exactly", "weight": "high"}],
  "editor_note": "verification concerns for the editor - never spoken"
}

Each piece feeds something downstream. Paragraphs go to the narrator, development labels go to on-screen boards, anchors tell the editor when to show them, and key phrases drive caption emphasis. The editor_note field gives the model a legitimate place to raise doubts, so it does not put them in the narration.

A retention structure

For a 60-70 second short, we ask for:

  1. Hook - one sentence, at most about 14 words, naming who and what, with tension and an open question. “X is facing renewed scrutiny” is weak; “X’s freedom now hangs on one ruling - and the court is under pressure” is a hook.
  2. Context - who, what and where, immediately.
  3. Two or three developments - distinct angles, each opening with a clear sentence.
  4. What happens next - a sharp ending with stakes. No “like and subscribe”.

Banned openings are enforced in code: if the first sentence contains “welcome”, “in this video” or a “what if” question, the script is rejected.

Target speaking time, not words

Our narrator speaks at about 126-157 words per minute, averaging around 140. A 65-second target therefore needs about 155 words. Models consistently under-write when given a word count, so we ask for sentences and paragraphs (“13-15 sentences across 5-6 paragraphs”) and validate the word count afterwards.

When a script is short, the rejection message tells the model exactly what to fix:

Narration has only 129 words; it needs 155 (add about 26 words: deepen the developments and the stakes with real context, no filler).

One repair attempt usually succeeds within seconds.

Factual boundaries, stated plainly

The prompt contains a short, non-negotiable block:

  • Never invent meetings, threats, resignations, documents, quotations, court decisions, numbers, dates or evidence.
  • Treat the headline as a reported development unless research notes confirm it, and attribute it (“reports say”, “according to claims circulating”).
  • Never describe an allegation as confirmed; keep analysis clearly labelled as analysis.
  • Well-established public background may be used.

Strong storytelling must come from the real stakes of the story, not from fabricated drama.

Guard-rails in code

Prompts reduce problems; validators catch what slips through.

Unsupported certainty. Without research notes, phrases such as “official reports confirm” or “sources confirmed that” are rejected, because nothing was confirmed. Correct negatives (“has not confirmed”) pass:

UNSUPPORTED = re.compile(r"(?i)(?<!not )\b((?:official(?:ly)?|reports?|sources?|it has been)\s+confirm(?:s|ed)?|"
                         r"confirm(?:s|ed)\s+that|now confirmed)\b")

Status downgrade. If the model marks a development “confirmed” but no research was supplied, the engine changes it to “reported”.

Anchors must exist. Every anchor phrase must appear verbatim in the narration, or the development cannot be timed.

Title relevance. At least one meaningful word from the headline must appear in the narration.

Flag what you cannot verify

Even a careful model, working from a headline alone, may add specifics such as a figure, a weekday, a court filing or “effective immediately”. These may be true background or may be invented. We cannot know without research, so the engine lists them for the editor:

Unverified specifics - check before posting:
- seventy thousand naira
- effective immediately
- file court papers

The list appears in the dashboard and as a quality warning. A human decides.

Optional research grounding

For stories where accuracy matters most, the engine can first gather short factual notes with a search-grounded model call. Each point is tied to a named publication and unconfirmed items are marked. The script must then use those notes for specifics. This is slower and can cost more, so it is a per-batch choice.

What the model never does

  • Choose the story or rewrite the headline.
  • Publish anything on its own.
  • Hide its doubts: they go to editor_note, which a person reads.

Takeaways

  • Ask for structured output that downstream code can verify.
  • Specify length as speaking time, and repair short answers with precise feedback.
  • Put factual rules in the prompt and in validators.
  • Surface unverifiable specifics instead of silently trusting them.

For the other half of the problem, removing AI meta-text before speech, see Stop AI Preambles From Reaching Your Text-to-Speech.
