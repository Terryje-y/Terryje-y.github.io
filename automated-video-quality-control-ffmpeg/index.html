Automated Quality Control for Generated Videos With ffprobe and FFmpeg

The 30 automated checks we run on generated videos before publishing: format, silence, black frames, captions, sync and leaked AI text.

Automation fails silently. A video can render without errors and still have a missing audio track, a three-second black gap, captions running off screen or a narrator reading a heading aloud. A person watching every output does not scale, so our engine runs about thirty automated checks on every video and blocks automatic publishing when a critical one fails. Here is how to build the same safety net.

Structure: critical checks and warnings

Each check records a name, a pass/fail result, whether it is critical, and a human-readable detail:

def check(name, ok, detail="", critical=True):
    checks.append({"check": name, "passed": bool(ok), "critical": critical, "detail": detail})

A video passes when every critical check passes. Warnings (for example, a short running 78 seconds instead of 60-70) are shown to the editor but do not block anything. The full report is saved as JSON next to the video and displayed in the dashboard.

1. Container and stream checks with ffprobe

ffprobe -v error -show_streams -show_format -of json video.mp4

From that JSON we verify:

  • dimensions match the format (1080×1920 for vertical, 1280×720 or 1920×1080 for widescreen);
  • H.264 video with yuv420p pixel format (the safest choice for platform uploads);
  • frame rate between 23 and 60 fps;
  • an AAC audio stream exists;
  • duration matches the narration plus a small tail, within 0.7 seconds.

These catch the classic automation bugs: a filter that changed resolution, a dropped audio map, a loop that ran too long.

2. Audio level and long silences

ffmpeg -i video.mp4 -af volumedetect -vn -f null -
ffmpeg -i video.mp4 -af silencedetect=noise=-45dB:d=2.5 -vn -f null -

We parse mean_volume from the first command and fail below −40 dB (effectively no narration). The second lists every silence longer than 2.5 seconds; any silence inside the narration fails the video, because it usually means a speech chunk failed to generate.

3. Black sections

ffmpeg -i video.mp4 -vf blackdetect=d=0.4:pic_th=0.97 -an -f null -

Any black_start … black_end interval fails the check. Black frames come from missing footage, a crossfade offset error or a broken overlay.

4. Narration timing

From the word timeline:

  • the timing method must be real alignment, not an estimate;
  • at least 70% of words must be matched directly;
  • the last word must end before the video ends.

5. Development board sync

For each development board, we look up its anchor phrase in the timeline and require that:

  • the board starts within 0.05 seconds of the anchor’s first word;
  • that first word carries a real recognised timestamp;
  • at least 60% of the anchor’s words were recognised.

Our first version demanded that every anchor word be recognised, and failed videos whose boards were perfectly timed because one later word was misheard. The check now tests what matters: the moment the board appears.

6. Leaked AI text

The final narration text is scanned for headings, stage directions, citation markers and meta lines such as “Here is your script”. One hit fails the video. See Stop AI Preambles From Reaching Your Text-to-Speech.

7. Caption layout

Captions are grouped exactly as the renderer groups them, and each chunk is wrapped with the same calibrated text-measuring function. The check fails if any chunk needs more than two lines or is wider than the safe area.

8. Visual sanity from sampled frames

We grab a frame and measure simple image statistics with Pillow:

frame = Image.open("sample.png").convert("L")
headline = frame.crop((40, 150, 1040, 486))
assert ImageStat.Stat(headline).stddev[0] > 25     # text present, not an empty box
band = frame.crop((0, 500, 1080, 1300))
assert ImageStat.Stat(band).mean[0] > 12           # footage visible, not black

Contrast in the headline region means text is actually drawn. For widescreen videos we check the lower news strip in the same way.

9. Assets, repetition and variety

  • every clip used must still exist, and every font file must be present;
  • no clip may appear twice in a row;
  • for the dynamic style, at least two kinds of creative graphic or two strong transitions, no single type dominating, and graphics covering at most half the runtime;
  • the background cuts must fall exactly every 3.0 seconds.

10. Publishing metadata

Before auto-publishing to TikTok: caption length within 2,200 UTF-16 units, three to eight relevant hashtags, and a privacy level chosen by the user. An “unverified specifics” warning lists figures or legal claims in the script that were not in the original headline and were not researched, so a human checks them before posting.

What happens on failure

  • Critical failure: the video is kept, marked “QC failed”, and automatic publishing is blocked. The editor sees exactly which checks failed.
  • Retry: re-running graphics and rendering is one click; script and narration are reused.
  • Check bugs are bugs too: when a check misfires (ours once ran a vertical-only headline check on a widescreen video), we fix the check, not the threshold.

Summary

Quality control for generated media is mostly a list of very simple measurements - stream metadata, volume, silence, black frames, text measurement and a few pixel statistics - applied without exception. The effort is small; the embarrassment it prevents is large.
