Building a 3-Second Montage With FFmpeg xfade (and the Transition That Crashed)

Build a 3-second news montage with FFmpeg xfade from Python: segment maths, crossfade chains, low-memory grouping and a crashing transition.

News AI Engine Editorial Team November 21, 2026 · 4 min read

Fast news edits have a rhythm: a new shot every few seconds keeps the eye engaged while the narration carries the story. Our engine cuts the background footage every three seconds, always. This article shows how to build that montage with FFmpeg’s xfade filter from Python, and the problems we solved along the way.

The arithmetic of a 3-second rhythm

We render each shot as a 3.5-second segment and join consecutive segments with a 0.5-second crossfade. Because each crossfade overlaps two segments by half a second, a new shot becomes dominant every 3.0 seconds.

For n segments, the total length is:

total = n × 3.5 − (n − 1) × 0.5 = 3n + 0.5

So the number of segments needed for a narration of length d is ceil((d − 3.5) / 3) + 1. A 65-second short needs 22 segments; a 20-minute video needs about 400.

Step 1: Normalise every segment

Each source - a video clip or a still image - is rendered to an identical intermediate: same size, frame rate, pixel format and duration. This makes the crossfade step simple and reliable.

def segment(src, out, w, h, start=None, is_image=False, focus_x=0.5):
    cmd = ["ffmpeg", "-y", "-loglevel", "error"]
    if is_image:
        cmd += ["-loop", "1", "-framerate", "30", "-i", src, "-t", "3.5"]
    else:
        cmd += ["-ss", f"{start:.3f}", "-i", src, "-t", "3.5", "-an"]
    vf = (f"scale={int(w*1.12)}:{int(h*1.12)}:force_original_aspect_ratio=increase:flags=lanczos,"
          f"crop={w}:{h}:(iw-ow)*{focus_x:.2f}:(ih-oh)/2,unsharp=5:5:0.6,fps=30,setsar=1,format=yuv420p")
    cmd += ["-vf", vf, "-c:v", "libx264", "-preset", "ultrafast", "-crf", "18", out]
    subprocess.run(cmd, check=True)

Three details worth copying:

  • Cover, then crop. force_original_aspect_ratio=increase fills the frame; the crop removes the overflow.
  • Crop around the subject. focus_x comes from a vision model’s estimate of where the main face is, so vertical crops do not cut people in half.
  • High-quality intermediates. CRF 18 with the ultrafast preset is large but fast, and avoids stacking compression artefacts across several passes.

Step 2: Chain the crossfades

xfade joins two inputs. Chaining it joins many. The offset for the i-th transition is the running length minus the overlap:

filters = [f"[{i}:v]settb=AVTB,setpts=PTS-STARTPTS,fps=30,format=yuv420p[v{i}]" for i in range(n)]
current, elapsed = "[v0]", 3.5
for i in range(1, n):
    offset = elapsed - 0.5
    filters.append(f"{current}[v{i}]xfade=transition={kinds[i-1]}:duration=0.5:offset={offset:.3f}[x{i}]")
    current, elapsed = f"[x{i}]", offset + 3.5

settb=AVTB,setpts=PTS-STARTPTS normalises timestamps, which prevents the most common xfade error: inputs with different time bases.

Step 3: Keep memory under control

The first version chained all segments in one filter graph. With 23 inputs that worked on a desktop, but on our phone the process was occasionally killed by Android’s low-memory killer, which leaves no error message - just a negative exit code.

The fix is to crossfade in groups of eight, then crossfade the groups. Because each join still overlaps by exactly 0.5 seconds, the result is mathematically identical:

group of 8 segments = 8 × 3.5 − 7 × 0.5 = 24.5 s
join offset         = 24.5 − 0.5 = 24.0 s = 8 × 3.0   ✓ same cadence

For long YouTube videos we go one level further and render the montage in roughly one-minute chunks, each with its own effects, and then join the chunks the same way. See Rendering Long YouTube Videos on an Android Phone.

We also made the runner retry once when FFmpeg exits with a negative return code (killed by a signal), with a short pause to let memory recover.

Step 4: Rhythm effects - freeze-zooms and black-and-white beats

A montage that only crossfades becomes monotonous. Two effects break the pattern:

  • Freeze-frame zoom. Every 24 seconds, starting at 12, we grab a frame, slowly push in on it for six seconds with zoompan, and overlay it on top of the montage. A small animated waveform appears during the freeze.
  • Black-and-white beats. A few four-second windows are desaturated with hue=s=0, enabled only within their time range:
hue=s=0:enable='between(t,31.40,35.40)'

Both effects are positioned with a fixed random seed per video, so re-rendering a video reproduces exactly the same edit.

The transition that crashed

Our AI Visual Director can choose different transitions at story turns: smooth slides for whips, zoomin for emphasis, wipes for broadcast style. One day a render failed with exit code −11: a segmentation fault.

We isolated it by testing each transition on two synthetic clips:

for k in fade dissolve smoothleft smoothright zoomin wipeleft slideleft slideup slidedown; do
  ffmpeg -v error -y -i a.mp4 -i b.mp4 -filter_complex \
    "[0:v]settb=AVTB,format=yuv420p[a];[1:v]settb=AVTB,format=yuv420p[b];[a][b]xfade=transition=$k:duration=0.5:offset=3" \
    -c:v libx264 -preset ultrafast out.mp4; echo "$k exit=$?"
done

Every transition passed except slideup, which crashed reliably on that FFmpeg build. The fix had two parts: remove it from the director’s options, and add a whitelist of transitions verified on the target machine. Anything not on the list falls back to fade. A creative choice should never be able to crash a render.

Choosing transitions with taste

Not every cut deserves a flashy transition. Our rules:

  • crossfades and dissolves within a thought;
  • a whip or zoom only at a real story turn, such as the start of a new development;
  • never the same strong transition twice in a row;
  • “classic” style uses fades only.

Summary

  • 3.5-second segments with 0.5-second crossfades give a clean 3-second cut rhythm.
  • Normalise every intermediate; reset timestamps before xfade.
  • Group crossfades to keep memory bounded - the timing does not change.
  • Test filters on your actual build, and whitelist what works.
