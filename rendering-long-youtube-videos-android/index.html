Rendering Long YouTube Videos on an Android Phone: Chunked Montages and Real Costs

How we render multi-minute 16:9 YouTube videos with synced graphics on a phone: chunked montages, bounded memory, resumable stages, real timings.

By News AI Engine Editorial Team Published November 21, 2026 4 min read

Our original YouTube renderer had a trick that saved time and cost quality: it built a two-minute montage once and looped it under the whole narration. A twelve-minute video showed the same two minutes of footage six times. When we moved YouTube onto the same smart editor as our shorts - word-synced captions, development boards and AI-planned graphics - we had to render genuinely long montages on a phone. This case study covers what we changed and what it costs.

The widescreen layout

Vertical shorts and widescreen videos share one graphics library, switched between a portrait and a landscape layout. In 16:9:

  • footage fills the frame, with a cut every three seconds throughout;
  • an opening title card shows the headline, then a persistent lower news strip carries it for the rest of the video - scrolling as a ticker when the title is long;
  • a small channel badge sits top-left;
  • captions sit just above the strip;
  • development boards, name supers and other graphics use the upper and left area, leaving the right of the frame to the footage.

Graphics are designed on a 1920×1080 canvas and libass scales them to the output resolution. We default to 720p and offer 1080p as an option.

Problem 1: A 400-input filter graph is not an option

A 20-minute narration needs about 400 three-second segments. Our short-form montage crossfaded segments in groups of eight and then joined the groups, which already bounds memory. The freeze-frame zoom and black-and-white effects, however, were applied in one pass over the whole montage - fine for a minute, not for twenty.

The solution is chunking at the effect level:

  1. Split the segment list into chunks of 20 segments (about one minute).
  2. Render each chunk completely - crossfades, freeze-zooms, black-and-white beats, vignette - with its own seed.
  3. Join the chunks with the same 0.5-second crossfades, in groups of eight.

Because every join overlaps by exactly half a second, the three-second cadence continues seamlessly across chunk boundaries. Peak memory depends on the chunk size, not on the video length.

Problem 2: Planning graphics for a long script

Our Visual Director asks a language model to plan graphics sentence by sentence. A long script has hundreds of sentences, which is too much for one request and too fragile to retry as a whole. The director now plans in windows of about 28 sentences. If a window fails because the provider is overloaded, the rest of the video uses the rule-based planner immediately, instead of waiting on the overloaded provider again for every window.

Graphic limits also scale with length: a one-minute short gets at most one full-screen punch line, a twelve-minute video several - still within the same share of runtime.

Problem 3: Development boards from the script itself

Long-form scripts already contain a structure: a development board listing the main developments, followed by sections for each. The editor uses that structure directly. It takes the board’s labels, finds the first words of each section in the narration, and times each board to the moment those words are spoken. No AI call is needed for the part that matters most. An optional AI pass adds names, places, numbers and key phrases for supers and emphasis.

Problem 4: Interruptions

A long render on a phone will be interrupted sometimes. Every stage writes its result to a cache folder:

story.json      developments and entities
timeline.json   word timings
clips.json      footage choice per 3-second slot
plan.json       visual plan
graphics.ass    generated motion graphics
montage.mp4     the long montage (deleted after the final render)

On restart, completed stages are loaded instead of recomputed. Re-editing a finished video in a different style reuses the word timeline.

What it really costs

On our test - 5.7 minutes of narration, dynamic style, 720p - the full run took 43 minutes of wall-clock time on the phone. That figure is honest rather than flattering:

  • about 10 minutes went to waiting on overloaded AI providers before falling back to rules;
  • the phone was also running other work during the test;
  • alignment ran at roughly real time, and the montage rendered at roughly one minute per chunk.

All 24 applicable quality checks passed once a vertical-only check was corrected. Our rule of thumb for planning is now two to three minutes of phone time per minute of 720p video, plus alignment. That is far slower than the old looped renderer, so the original editor remains available when speed matters more than polish.

Storage

A 20-minute 720p video at CRF 21 is a few hundred megabytes, and the temporary montage is a similar size. The editor estimates the space it needs before starting and refuses to begin a render that would fill the device.

Lessons

  • Loops are a false economy. Viewers notice repeated footage long before they notice a slightly softer encode.
  • Chunk at the level where the cost grows. For us that was the effects pass, not only the crossfades.
  • Let structure do the important work. Boards timed from the script’s own sections need no model and never hallucinate.
  • Publish your real numbers. Planning a channel around an optimistic render time is how pipelines fall behind.
